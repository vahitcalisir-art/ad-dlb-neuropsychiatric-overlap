# Introduction

Most patients with dementia develop at least some neuropsychiatric symptoms during the disease, although the prevalence and persistence of individual symptoms vary widely [@zhu2026neuropsychiatric#c1] <!--id:intro_nps-->. About 85% of patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD) will develop a neuropsychiatric symptom at some point in the illness [@kumar2026genetic#c7]. In a longitudinal clinical cohort, depressed mood was reported by 51% of participants with AD and by 65.2% of those with clinically diagnosed dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), in whom psychosis was also frequent [@zhu2026neuropsychiatric#c2; @zhu2026neuropsychiatric#c3; @zhu2026neuropsychiatric#c7]. Psychosis in DLB was associated with faster functional decline [@zhu2026neuropsychiatric#c6]. Depressive symptoms are common in DLB, yet trial data on their treatment are scant [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c2]. Symptoms can also precede the diagnosis: mild behavioral impairment, defined by later-life onset and persistence for at least six months, carried a pooled hazard ratio of 2.70 for incident dementia [@sassi2026mild#c4; @sassi2026mild#c1]. Heterogeneity across studies was substantial, and the association was not specific to AD [@sassi2026mild#c2; @sassi2026mild#c6].

Psychiatric disorders that begin earlier in life are also associated with later dementia [@livingston2024dementia#c1; @livingston2024dementia#c8] <!--id:intro_psych-->. The 2024 Lancet Commission found a higher risk of dementia 10 to 14 years after a diagnosis of depression and concluded that depression increases risk at all adult ages, although part of the late-life association reflects preclinical dementia [@livingston2024dementia#c4; @livingston2024dementia#c1]. A meta-analysis suggested that post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a risk factor for dementia, but with considerable heterogeneity between studies [@livingston2024dementia#c6]. The Commission therefore judged the evidence too heterogeneous to classify PTSD as a modifiable risk factor [@livingston2024dementia#c5]. People with schizophrenia (SCZ) develop more and earlier dementia than others, but it is unclear whether SCZ predisposes to dementia independently of the other risk factors that accompany it [@livingston2024dementia#c8; @livingston2024dementia#c9]. For insomnia, a meta-analysis reported higher risks of all-cause dementia and AD [@meng2025insomnia#c1], whereas a Norwegian cohort found no association with either [@livingston2024dementia#c7]. In that meta-analysis the association was stronger with less than five years of follow-up than with longer follow-up [@meng2025insomnia#c2]. Such a pattern is expected when risks identified in the few years before dementia reflect reverse causation as well as, or instead of, a causal effect [@livingston2024dementia#c2] <!--id:intro_revcaus-->. Neuropsychiatric symptoms frequently emerge years before measurable cognitive impairment [@sassi2026mild#c3], and the relationship between insomnia and dementia is considered bidirectional [@meng2025insomnia#c4].

Genetic data offer two partly independent approaches: genome-wide genetic correlation (rg) can be estimated by linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (LDSC) from genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics alone, and it is not biased by sample overlap [@buliksullivan2015atlas#c1]. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instruments, and its estimates reflect the lifetime effect of genetic liability to the exposure [@skrivankova2021strengthening2#c7; @skrivankova2021strengthening2#c8]. Bidirectional MR additionally assesses the direction of a causal association [@skrivankova2021strengthening2#c4]. Earlier genome-wide analyses found no genetic correlation between AD and major depressive disorder (MDD) [@gibson2017assessing#c1; @monereosanchez2021genetic#c1] <!--id:intro_null-->. An early atlas of genetic correlations concluded that the genetic basis of AD is distinct from psychiatric conditions [@buliksullivan2015atlas#c8]. A recent study again reported genome-wide correlations near zero between AD and both depression and SCZ [@kumar2026genetic#c2]. MR has likewise found no causal effect of sleep traits, including insomnia, or of MDD on AD [@huang2020sleep#c2; @huang2020sleep#c9]. In the reverse direction, genetic liability to AD was associated with a lower risk of insomnia, but reverse-direction associations of this kind depended on the APOE ε4 allele [@huang2020sleep#c1; @huang2020sleep#c7].

Null genome-wide results do not exclude shared genetic effects, because local correlations in opposing directions can cancel out in a genome-wide estimate [@werme2022integrated#c1] <!--id:intro_local-->. Bivariate mixture modelling found that a large share of the causal variants for AD overlapped with depression despite a negligible genome-wide correlation [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c4; @monereosanchez2021genetic#c5]. Local genetic correlations also show greater heterogeneity among psychiatric traits than genome-wide estimates suggest [@nievergelt2024genome#c8]. A local analysis with the Local Analysis of [co]Variant Annotation (LAVA) method reported 17 loci shared between AD and depression and 23 between AD and SCZ [@kumar2026genetic#c3], but at an uncorrected threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻³ [@kumar2026genetic#c5]. The APOE region raises a different problem, because its pleiotropy violates the exclusion restriction when APOE variants are used as instruments for AD [@huang2020sleep#c6]. APOE ε4 is also the major common genetic risk factor for DLB, and whether it acts mainly through coexisting AD pathology is unresolved [@watkins2026understanding#c2; @watkins2026understanding#c5]. Genetic data on DLB are limited: its largest GWAS included 2,591 cases, against more than 60,000 in recent AD GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1], and it had limited power for common variants of small effect [@chia2021genome#c8] <!--id:intro_dlb-->. DLB is often missed or misdiagnosed as AD, and mixed AD and Lewy-related pathology is common [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c3; @mckeith2017diagnosis#c4]. The largest AD GWAS, for its part, combines clinically diagnosed cases with proxy cases defined by parental history [@bellenguez2022new#c4; @bellenguez2022new#c2]. Proxy-based AD GWAS can give misleading genetic correlations [@wu2024pervasive#c1]. In proxy GWAS of AD based on parental history, the component unrelated to AD correlated negatively with MDD and SCZ [@wu2024pervasive#c4], and survival bias is expected to produce spurious negative correlations with other health outcomes [@wu2024pervasive#c5]. FinnGen, in contrast, defines AD from register-based diagnosis codes and is enriched for AD cases [@kurki2023finngen#c4; @kurki2023finngen#c7].

The present study examined MDD, PTSD, insomnia and SCZ against AD and DLB at three levels, namely genome-wide rg [@buliksullivan2015atlas#c1], local rg with LAVA [@werme2022integrated#c1] and bidirectional two-sample MR [@hemani2018mr#c1], with APOE-excluded analyses and a clinically diagnosed AD GWAS without proxy cases as sensitivity analyses [@huang2020sleep#c6; @wu2024pervasive#c6] <!--id:intro_aim-->.
