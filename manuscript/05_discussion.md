# Discussion

Genome-wide, local and MR analyses gave no robust evidence of genetic overlap between MDD, PTSD, insomnia or SCZ and AD (Figures 2–4). Genome-wide correlations with AD were close to zero, and their upper confidence limits excluded positive values above {{res:power_ldsc.ad.ptsd.excluded_above}} to {{res:power_ldsc.ad.scz.excluded_above}}. None of the {{res:lava_ukb.summary.bivar_tests_primary}} testable local correlations passed FDR correction. Of the sixteen MR directions, only PTSD → AD passed FDR correction, and it did not withstand the pre-specified pleiotropy and overlap checks (Table 2). For DLB the picture differed: genome-wide correlations with MDD and PTSD passed FDR correction at the margin (FDR-corrected P {{res:ldsc_rg.main.dlb.mdd.p_fdr}} for both), but on a DLB heritability flagged as low power (Z {{res:ldsc_h2.main.dlb.h2_z}}).

The AD results agree with earlier LDSC analyses, which found no genetic correlation between clinically diagnosed AD and MDD [@gibson2017assessing#c1]. A UK Biobank-free analysis reported a negligible correlation with depression [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c1], and an early atlas of genetic correlations concluded that the genetic basis of AD is distinct from psychiatric conditions [@buliksullivan2015atlas#c8]. A recent study using the same AD and SCZ summary statistics as the present one reported genome-wide correlations near zero for depression and SCZ [@kumar2026genetic#c2; @kumar2026genetic#c9; @kumar2026genetic#c10]. Concordance with that study is therefore not independent replication [@kumar2026genetic#c9]. Null genome-wide estimates can still coexist with substantial polygenic overlap when effects run in both directions [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c4; @monereosanchez2021genetic#c5].

Local analysis was intended to detect such opposing signals, which can cancel in a genome-wide estimate [@werme2022integrated#c1; @werme2022integrated#c3]. With the UK Biobank reference, few loci carried local heritability in both traits, and the nominal AD × MDD correlations were split in sign ({{res:lava_exploratory.ad.mdd.nominal_positive_rho}} positive, {{res:lava_exploratory.ad.mdd.nominal_negative_rho}} negative). These loci did not pass FDR correction and are exploratory (Supplementary Table S5). Kumar et al. reported 17 local AD–depression and 23 local AD–SCZ loci at an uncorrected threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻³, using the same LAVA locus partition [@kumar2026genetic#c3; @kumar2026genetic#c5; @kumar2026genetic#c11]. The difference from the present null result can reflect the threshold, the multiple-testing correction and the LD reference, which that report does not specify for the local analysis [@kumar2026genetic#c11].

The reference panel mattered: with the 1000 Genomes panel, {{res:lava_g1000_fdr_pairs.summary.n_fdr_pairs_g1000}} primary locus–pairs passed FDR correction, but {{res:lava_g1000_fdr_pairs.summary.n_not_tested_ukb}} of them were not testable with the UK Biobank reference, and summed local heritability was higher with 1000 Genomes for every trait ({{res:lava_reference_diagnostic.supplementary.range}}-fold; Supplementary Table S7). The LAVA developers recommend the UK Biobank reference for European-ancestry data because the smaller 1000 Genomes panel may inflate type 1 error and bias local heritability [@werme2025lava#c1; @werme2025lava#c2]. The pattern observed here is consistent with that concern (Supplementary Table S7). The recommendation rests on a report that has not been peer reviewed, and the present data cannot establish inflation, so the 1000 Genomes results are treated as a sensitivity analysis and not as findings (Supplementary Table S6). The chromosome 17 loci containing MAPT and KANSL1 gave the same sign with both references (Supplementary Table S6), but they remained nominal and are not interpreted biologically.

The inverse PTSD → AD estimate (OR {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.or_ivw}}) requires caution. Its classification as sensitive to pleiotropy followed from the SIMEX MR-Egger intercept (P {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.simex_intercept_p}}), and the overlap-corrected MRlap estimate was not nominally significant (P {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.p_mrlap_corrected}}). With FinnGen AD as outcome, the estimate was not significant and its confidence interval was wide (OR {{res:sensitivity_finngen.mr.ptsd__ad_finngen.or_ivw}}). Power was lower in FinnGen, with a minimum detectable OR of {{res:mr_ptsd_ad_finngen.ptsd__ad_finngen.min_detectable_or_plan}} against {{res:mr_ptsd_ad_finngen.ptsd__ad.min_detectable_or_plan}} with the primary AD GWAS.

A bias in the primary AD GWAS offers one possible explanation, because proxy-based AD GWAS can give misleading genetic correlations [@wu2024pervasive#c1]. In proxy GWAS of AD based on parental history, the non-AD component was negatively correlated with MDD and SCZ, reported as "cor. = −0.11, P = 0.012" and "cor. = −0.13, P = 8.3 × 10⁻³" [@wu2024pervasive#c4]. Without adjustment for survival bias, spurious negative genetic correlations between proxy AD GWAS and other health outcomes are expected [@wu2024pervasive#c5]. The primary AD GWAS combined clinical and proxy cases without releasing them separately [@wu2024pervasive#c6; @bellenguez2022new#c3], and meta-analysis alone does not remove this bias [@wu2024pervasive#c7]. That study reported no value for PTSD [@wu2024pervasive#c4]. Extending the bias to PTSD is therefore an inference: given the genetic overlap of PTSD with MDD [@nievergelt2024genome#c7], an inverse estimate would be consistent with it. FinnGen is not free of bias either, because its AD status comes from diagnostic codes and its controls were not age-matched, which may exacerbate survival bias [@wu2024pervasive#c8]. These data provide no robust causal evidence for an effect of PTSD liability on AD (Table 2; Figure 4).

The FinnGen and primary AD GWAS also differed in their genome-wide correlation with PTSD ({{res:sensitivity_finngen.ldsc.finngen.ptsd.rg}} against {{res:ldsc_rg.main.ad.ptsd.rg}}). The FinnGen estimate did not survive correction across four tests (BH-adjusted P {{res:sensitivity_finngen.ldsc.finngen.ptsd.p_bh_within4}}), so chance cannot be excluded. Several explanations are possible for this difference (Figure 2), and none is established. FinnGen derives from a founder population [@kurki2023finngen#c3], and European LD scores applied to Finnish samples can bias LDSC [@buliksullivan2015ld#c7]. Its register-based AD diagnosis differs from the clinical and proxy definition of the primary AD GWAS [@kurki2023finngen#c4; @bellenguez2022new#c3]. FinnGen also contributes to the PTSD GWAS [@nievergelt2024genome#c3]. Proxy and survival biases may affect both AD GWAS through different mechanisms [@wu2024pervasive#c4; @wu2024pervasive#c8].

The other MR directions were consistent with earlier work (Figure 4). Genetic liability to MDD showed no association with AD (OR {{res:mr.mdd__ad.or_ivw}}), and genetic liability to insomnia showed none either (OR {{res:mr.insomnia__ad.or_ivw}}). An earlier MR study that tested insomnia among other sleep phenotypes also found no causal effect of sleep patterns or MDD on AD [@huang2020sleep#c2; @huang2020sleep#c9]. For AD → insomnia, the MRlap-corrected estimate was nominally inverse ({{res:mr.ad__insomnia.beta_std_mrlap_corrected}}), in the same direction as an earlier report of AD liability and lower insomnia risk [@huang2020sleep#c1]. Earlier reverse-direction associations depended on APOE ε4 [@huang2020sleep#c7], and the APOE-excluded AD → insomnia estimate here was {{res:mr.ad__insomnia__noapoe.or_ivw}}. Genetic liability to AD showed no association with MDD (OR {{res:mr.ad__mdd.or_ivw}}). The SCZ → AD association was nominal only and did not pass FDR correction (FDR-corrected P {{res:mr.scz__ad.p_ivw_fdr}}).

The DLB correlations with MDD and PTSD are borderline and rest on weak data (Figure 2). The DLB GWAS had limited power for common variants of small effect [@chia2021genome#c8] and includes 2,591 cases, against more than 60,000 in recent AD GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1]. DLB is often missed or misdiagnosed as AD, and mixed pathology is common [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c3; @mckeith2017diagnosis#c4]. Depressive symptoms are common in DLB [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c2; @watkins2026understanding#c6], so shared clinical features or misclassification could contribute to a genetic correlation. Neither explanation can be tested here, and the estimates need confirmation in a larger DLB GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1] <!--id:disc_dlb_confirm-->. MR could not address the DLB question: the minimum detectable ORs for psychiatric exposures on DLB ranged from {{res:mr.mdd__dlb.min_detectable_or}} to {{res:mr.insomnia__dlb.min_detectable_or}} (Table 2). Steiger filtering removed many MDD instruments for DLB, but halving or doubling the prevalences {{res:text.steiger_prevalence_change}} (Supplementary Table S9).

The data do not support a causal effect of genetic liability to MDD, PTSD, insomnia or SCZ on AD (Table 2; Figure 4). This would be consistent with the view that part of the late-life association between depression and dementia reflects preclinical dementia [@livingston2024dementia#c1]. Neuropsychiatric symptoms can emerge years before measurable cognitive impairment [@sassi2026mild#c3], so their observational association with dementia does not by itself show that psychiatric liability leads to AD [@livingston2024dementia#c2]. For DLB, the MR analyses had low power, and the absence of association there is not informative (Table 2).

Among the strengths of the design, hypotheses, thresholds and an interpretation rule were fixed in an analysis plan frozen before cross-trait analyses, and every departure is documented (Supplementary Table S1). The primary MDD file excluded UK Biobank, which removed overlap with the primary AD GWAS by design (Table 1). Three analytic layers were combined, and a register-based AD GWAS without proxy cases (FinnGen), which also excluded UK Biobank, served as a sensitivity analysis (Figure 4). The local analysis used fixed per-pair seeds so that it can be reproduced exactly <!--depends:GATE3_RESULT--> (Supplementary Table S1).

All data were of European ancestry, and studies of European samples miss variation that matters in other populations [@auton2015global#c8]. Sample overlap through UK Biobank could not be removed for PTSD and insomnia [@bellenguez2022new#c5; @nievergelt2024genome#c3], and overlap biases MR toward the observational estimate [@mounier2023bias#c3]. The primary AD GWAS includes proxy cases with lower specificity than a clinical diagnosis [@bellenguez2022new#c3; @bellenguez2022new#c8]. The psychiatric phenotypes were broad: depression cases were partly self-reported [@adams2025trans#c4], and PTSD cases were defined from diagnostic codes in health records [@nievergelt2024genome#c4]. MR estimates reflect lifetime genetic liability [@skrivankova2021strengthening2#c7; @skrivankova2021strengthening2#c8], whereas psychiatric symptoms in the years before dementia may be prodromal [@livingston2024dementia#c2; @livingston2024dementia#c3]. Selective survival can bias all instruments in the same way and is not addressed by robust estimators [@bowden2016consistent#c7], which matters for SCZ, a trait with reduced life expectancy [@trubetskoy2022mapping#c7]. Weak instruments bias two-sample estimates toward the null [@bowden2015mendelian#c8], so small effects cannot be excluded where power was low (Table 2).

Future work would benefit from clinically diagnosed AD GWAS without proxy cases, or from separate release of case–control and proxy results [@wu2024pervasive#c6; @wu2024pervasive#c7]. A substantially larger DLB GWAS is needed before its genetic correlations with MDD and PTSD can be interpreted [@watkins2026understanding#c1]. Narrower depression subtypes may show more concordant effects with AD than broad depression [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c7], and late-life psychiatric phenotypes would address the question of prodromal symptoms more directly than lifetime liability, because part of the late-life association between depression and dementia reflects preclinical dementia [@livingston2024dementia#c1].
