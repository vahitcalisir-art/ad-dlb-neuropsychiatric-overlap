# Discussion

Across genome-wide, local and MR analyses, there was no robust evidence of genetic overlap between AD and MDD, PTSD, insomnia or SCZ (Figures 2–4). Genome-wide correlations with AD were close to zero, with upper confidence limits that excluded positive values above {{res:power_ldsc.ad.ptsd.excluded_above}} to {{res:power_ldsc.ad.scz.excluded_above}}, and none of the {{res:lava_ukb.summary.bivar_tests_primary}} testable local correlations passed FDR correction. PTSD → AD was the only one of sixteen MR directions to pass FDR correction, and it did not withstand the pre-specified pleiotropy and overlap checks (Table 2). DLB behaved differently, with genome-wide correlations with MDD and PTSD that passed FDR correction at the margin (FDR-corrected P {{res:ldsc_rg.main.dlb.mdd.p_fdr}} for both), on a heritability flagged as low power (Z {{res:ldsc_h2.main.dlb.h2_z}}).

These AD results agree with earlier LDSC analyses that found no genetic correlation between clinically diagnosed AD and MDD [@gibson2017assessing#c1] and with a UK Biobank-free analysis reporting a negligible correlation with depression [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c1]. The first atlas of LDSC correlations likewise found the genetic basis of AD to be distinct from that of psychiatric conditions [@buliksullivan2015atlas#c8]. A recent study reported near-zero genome-wide correlations of AD with depression and SCZ using the same AD and SCZ summary statistics as the present one [@kumar2026genetic#c2; @kumar2026genetic#c9; @kumar2026genetic#c10], so agreement with it is not independent replication [@kumar2026genetic#c9]. Null genome-wide estimates can nonetheless coexist with substantial polygenic overlap when effects run in both directions [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c4; @monereosanchez2021genetic#c5].

The local analysis was meant to detect such opposing signals, which can cancel in a genome-wide estimate [@werme2022integrated#c1; @werme2022integrated#c3]. With the UK Biobank reference, local heritability in both traits was uncommon, and the nominal AD × MDD correlations were split in sign ({{res:lava_exploratory.ad.mdd.nominal_positive_rho}} positive, {{res:lava_exploratory.ad.mdd.nominal_negative_rho}} negative). These loci did not pass FDR correction and remain exploratory (Supplementary Table S5). Kumar et al. reported 17 local AD–depression and 23 local AD–SCZ loci at an uncorrected threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻³ with the same LAVA locus partition [@kumar2026genetic#c3; @kumar2026genetic#c5; @kumar2026genetic#c11]. The difference from the present null result may reflect the threshold, the multiple-testing correction and the LD reference, which that report does not specify for its local analysis [@kumar2026genetic#c11].

With the 1000 Genomes panel, {{res:lava_g1000_fdr_pairs.summary.n_fdr_pairs_g1000}} primary locus–pairs passed FDR correction, yet {{res:lava_g1000_fdr_pairs.summary.n_not_tested_ukb}} of them were not testable with the UK Biobank reference, and summed local heritability was higher with 1000 Genomes for every trait ({{res:lava_reference_diagnostic.supplementary.range}}-fold; Supplementary Table S7). The LAVA developers recommend the UK Biobank reference for European-ancestry data because the smaller 1000 Genomes panel may inflate type 1 error and bias local heritability [@werme2025lava#c1; @werme2025lava#c2], and the pattern observed here is consistent with that concern (Supplementary Table S7). Their recommendation rests on a report that has not been peer reviewed, and the present data cannot establish inflation, so the 1000 Genomes results are treated as a sensitivity analysis and not as findings (Supplementary Table S6). The chromosome 17 loci containing MAPT and KANSL1 kept the same sign with both references (Supplementary Table S6) but remained nominal and are not interpreted biologically.

The inverse PTSD → AD estimate (OR {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.or_ivw}}) requires caution, because its classification as sensitive to pleiotropy followed from the SIMEX MR-Egger intercept (P {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.simex_intercept_p}}) and the overlap-corrected MRlap estimate was not nominally significant (P {{res:mr.ptsd__ad.p_mrlap_corrected}}). With FinnGen AD as outcome, the estimate was not significant and had a wide confidence interval (OR {{res:sensitivity_finngen.mr.ptsd__ad_finngen.or_ivw}}), and power was lower there, with a minimum detectable OR of {{res:mr_ptsd_ad_finngen.ptsd__ad_finngen.min_detectable_or_plan}} against {{res:mr_ptsd_ad_finngen.ptsd__ad.min_detectable_or_plan}} with the primary AD GWAS.

One possible explanation is bias in the primary AD GWAS, since proxy-based AD GWAS can give misleading genetic correlations [@wu2024pervasive#c1]. In proxy GWAS of AD based on parental history, the non-AD component correlated negatively with MDD and SCZ, reported as "cor. = −0.11, P = 0.012" and "cor. = −0.13, P = 8.3 × 10⁻³" [@wu2024pervasive#c4]. Without adjustment for survival bias, spurious negative genetic correlations with other health outcomes are expected [@wu2024pervasive#c5]. The primary AD GWAS combined clinical and proxy cases without releasing them separately [@wu2024pervasive#c6; @bellenguez2022new#c3], and meta-analysis alone does not remove the bias [@wu2024pervasive#c7]. Because that study reported no value for PTSD [@wu2024pervasive#c4], extending the bias to PTSD is an inference, and an inverse estimate would be consistent with it given the genetic overlap of PTSD with MDD [@nievergelt2024genome#c7]. FinnGen is not free of bias either, as its AD status comes from diagnostic codes and its controls were not age-matched, which may exacerbate survival bias [@wu2024pervasive#c8]. These data provide no robust causal evidence for an effect of PTSD liability on AD (Table 2; Figure 4).

The two AD GWAS also differed in their genome-wide correlation with PTSD ({{res:sensitivity_finngen.ldsc.finngen.ptsd.rg}} with FinnGen against {{res:ldsc_rg.main.ad.ptsd.rg}} with the primary AD GWAS). Because the FinnGen estimate did not survive correction across four tests (BH-adjusted P {{res:sensitivity_finngen.ldsc.finngen.ptsd.p_bh_within4}}), chance cannot be excluded. Several explanations are possible for this difference, and none is established (Figure 2). FinnGen derives from a founder population [@kurki2023finngen#c3], and European LD scores applied to Finnish samples can bias LDSC [@buliksullivan2015ld#c7]. Its register-based AD diagnosis differs from the clinical and proxy definition of the primary AD GWAS [@kurki2023finngen#c4; @bellenguez2022new#c3]. FinnGen also contributes to the PTSD GWAS [@nievergelt2024genome#c3]. Proxy and survival biases may affect both AD GWAS through different mechanisms [@wu2024pervasive#c4; @wu2024pervasive#c8].

The remaining MR directions agreed with earlier work (Figure 4). Neither MDD nor insomnia liability was associated with AD (OR {{res:mr.mdd__ad.or_ivw}} and {{res:mr.insomnia__ad.or_ivw}}), in line with an earlier MR study that tested insomnia among other sleep phenotypes and found no causal effect of sleep patterns or MDD on AD [@huang2020sleep#c2; @huang2020sleep#c9]. For AD → insomnia, the MRlap-corrected estimate was nominally inverse ({{res:mr.ad__insomnia.beta_std_mrlap_corrected}}), in the direction of an earlier report linking AD liability to a lower risk of insomnia [@huang2020sleep#c1]. In that report, reverse-direction associations with sleep duration depended on APOE ε4 [@huang2020sleep#c7], and the APOE-excluded AD → insomnia estimate here was {{res:mr.ad__insomnia__noapoe.or_ivw}}. AD liability showed no association with MDD (OR {{res:mr.ad__mdd.or_ivw}}), and the SCZ → AD association was nominal only, without passing FDR correction (FDR-corrected P {{res:mr.scz__ad.p_ivw_fdr}}).

The DLB correlations with MDD and PTSD are borderline and rest on weak data (Figure 2). The DLB GWAS had limited power for common variants of small effect [@chia2021genome#c8] and included 2,591 cases, against more than 60,000 in recent AD GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1]. Many DLB cases are missed or misdiagnosed as AD, and mixed pathology is common [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c3; @mckeith2017diagnosis#c4]. Because depressive symptoms are common in DLB [@mckeith2017diagnosis#c2; @watkins2026understanding#c6], shared clinical features or misclassification could contribute to a genetic correlation. Neither explanation can be tested here, and the estimates need confirmation in a larger DLB GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1] <!--id:disc_dlb_confirm-->. MR was not informative for DLB, with minimum detectable ORs for psychiatric exposures ranging from {{res:mr.mdd__dlb.min_detectable_or}} to {{res:mr.insomnia__dlb.min_detectable_or}} (Table 2). Steiger filtering removed many MDD instruments for DLB, but halving or doubling the prevalences {{res:text.steiger_prevalence_change}} (Supplementary Table S9).

These results do not support a causal effect of genetic liability to MDD, PTSD, insomnia or SCZ on AD (Table 2; Figure 4). Such a pattern would be consistent with the view that part of the late-life association between depression and dementia reflects preclinical dementia [@livingston2024dementia#c1]. Neuropsychiatric symptoms can emerge years before measurable cognitive impairment [@sassi2026mild#c3], so their observational association with dementia does not by itself show that psychiatric liability leads to AD [@livingston2024dementia#c2]. The inference does not extend to DLB, for which the MR analyses had low power (Table 2).

A strength of the study is that hypotheses, thresholds and an interpretation rule were fixed in an analysis plan frozen before cross-trait analyses, with every departure documented (Supplementary Table S1). Overlap between the MDD and AD GWAS was removed by design, because the primary MDD file excluded UK Biobank (Table 1). Three analytic layers were combined, and a register-based AD GWAS without proxy cases (FinnGen), which also excluded UK Biobank, served as a sensitivity analysis (Figure 4). Fixed per-pair seeds allow the local analysis to be reproduced exactly <!--depends:GATE3_RESULT--> (Supplementary Table S1).

All data were of European ancestry, and studies of European samples miss variation that matters in other populations [@auton2015global#c8]. Sample overlap through UK Biobank could not be removed for PTSD and insomnia [@bellenguez2022new#c5; @nievergelt2024genome#c3], and such overlap biases MR toward the observational estimate [@mounier2023bias#c3]. Proxy cases in the primary AD GWAS have lower specificity than a clinical diagnosis [@bellenguez2022new#c3; @bellenguez2022new#c8]. Depression cases were partly self-reported [@adams2025trans#c4] and PTSD cases were defined from diagnostic codes in health records [@nievergelt2024genome#c4], so both psychiatric phenotypes were broad. MR estimates reflect lifetime genetic liability [@skrivankova2021strengthening2#c7; @skrivankova2021strengthening2#c8], whereas psychiatric symptoms in the years before dementia may be prodromal [@livingston2024dementia#c2; @livingston2024dementia#c3]. The estimates also lack a straightforward causal interpretation where it is unclear whether differences in the exposure between genetically defined subgroups are equivalent to differences produced by an intervention [@skrivankova2021strengthening2#c10]. Selective survival can bias all instruments in the same way and is not addressed by robust estimators [@bowden2016consistent#c7], which matters for SCZ, a trait with reduced life expectancy [@trubetskoy2022mapping#c7]. Weak instruments bias two-sample estimates toward the null [@bowden2015mendelian#c8], so small effects cannot be excluded where power was low (Table 2).

Future studies would benefit from clinically diagnosed AD GWAS without proxy cases, or from separate release of case–control and proxy results [@wu2024pervasive#c6; @wu2024pervasive#c7]. Interpreting the DLB correlations with MDD and PTSD will require a substantially larger DLB GWAS [@watkins2026understanding#c1]. Narrower depression subtypes may show more concordant effects with AD than broad depression [@monereosanchez2021genetic#c7], and late-life psychiatric phenotypes would address prodromal symptoms more directly than lifetime liability [@livingston2024dementia#c1].
