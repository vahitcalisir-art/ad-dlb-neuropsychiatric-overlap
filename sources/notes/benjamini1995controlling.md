Benjamini Y, Hochberg Y; 1995; Controlling the false discovery rate: a practical and powerful approach to multiple testing; Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B (Methodological) 57(1):289–300; DOI 10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x; methodological paper (new error-rate definition, procedure with proof, simulation study, worked example); access route: published version, PDF supplied by the investigator (institutional library access), retrieved 2026-11-21

## Section-by-section reading log
- Abstract: (SUMMARY) Points out faults of controlling the familywise error rate (FWER) and proposes controlling the expected proportion of falsely rejected hypotheses, the false discovery rate (FDR); FDR equals FWER when all nulls are true and is smaller otherwise, so power can be gained; a simple sequential Bonferroni-type procedure is proved to control the FDR for independent test statistics; simulation shows substantial power gain; illustrated with examples.
- Introduction: (Section 1) Multiplicity inflates false positives; classical MCPs control FWER in the strong sense but are underused (medical literature surveys); difficulties: methodology tied to multivariate normal statistics, low power relative to per-comparison procedures, and FWER control often not needed (e.g. multiple end points); defines the FDR as the expected proportion of errors among rejected hypotheses, building on Spjøtvoll (1972) and Sorić (1989). Section 2 (false discovery rate): Table 1 (U, V, T, S, R by truth/decision); PCER = E(V/m), FWER = P(V ≥ 1); 2.1 defines Q = V/(V+S) with Q = 0 when nothing is rejected and FDR Qe = E(Q); properties: FDR = FWER when all nulls true (weak FWER control), FDR ≤ FWER otherwise, so power gain grows with the number of non-true nulls; 2.2 examples (multiple end points, multiple subgroups, screening); 2.3 alternative formulations (E(V/R | R>0), Sorić's E(V)/r, E(V)/E(R)) rejected as uncontrollable when all nulls are true.
- Methods: (Section 3 and Appendix A) 3.1 procedure (1): order p-values P(1) ≤ … ≤ P(m), let k be the largest i with P(i) ≤ (i/m)q*, reject H(1)…H(k); Theorem 1: for independent test statistics and any configuration of false nulls the procedure controls FDR at q* (in fact at (m0/m)q*), via a lemma proved by induction on m in Appendix A; remark that independence is not required among statistics of false nulls; relation to Simes (1986), Hommel (1988) and Hochberg (1988) — constants (i/m)q* always at least Hochberg's α/(m+1−i), so at least as many rejections as Hochberg and Holm. 3.2 worked example: Neuhaus et al. (1992) rt-PA vs APSAC trial, four hypothesis families; in the 15-hypothesis family Bonferroni (0.05/15 = 0.0033) and Hochberg reject 3, BH at q* = 0.05 rejects 4 (P(4) = 0.0095 ≤ 0.013), including mortality. 3.3 Theorem 2: the procedure maximizes the number of rejections r(α) subject to αm/r(α) ≤ q*.
- Results: (Section 4, simulation) m = 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 independent normal means tested by z-tests, q* = α = 0.05, proportion of true nulls 3/4, 1/2, 1/4, 0, non-null means at L/4…L in decreasing/equal/increasing configurations, L = 5 or 10, 20000 repetitions (SE 0.0008–0.0016); Fig. 1 average power: power falls with m for all methods; BH uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni and Hochberg; advantage increases with the number of non-nulls and with m; e.g. 32 hypotheses all false: Bonferroni 0.42 vs BH 0.65; 4 hypotheses half true: 0.62 vs 0.70; gain of FDR over FWER control much larger than Hochberg over Bonferroni; E(V/m0) under all-null close to q*/m0.
- Discussion: (Section 5, Conclusion) FDR control is philosophically different from strong FWER control, also gives weak FWER control and is the desirable control in many applications; other procedures (pairwise comparisons, adaptive method after Hochberg and Benjamini 1990) can be developed; the cost of multiplicity control need not be large.
- Limitations stated by authors: proof of Theorem 1 holds for independent test statistics (independence among true-null statistics required); simulation restricted to independent normal z-tests; the extension to structured problems (e.g. pairwise comparisons) and adaptive versions left to future work.
- Supplementary material: none; Appendix A (proof of the lemma) is part of the article and was read.

## Quality appraisal
Seminal, peer-reviewed methodological paper with a formal proof (Theorem 1, lemma in Appendix A) and a large simulation (20000 repetitions per configuration). The guarantee is proved for independent test statistics only; dependence (positive regression dependence) was addressed later by Benjamini and Yekutieli (2001), not in this paper. Terminology note: the paper calls procedure (1) "step-down" because it scans from the largest p-value downward; in current usage this is the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure (as implemented, e.g., in R p.adjust method "BH"). OCR of the scanned PDF garbles inequality symbols (≤ rendered as "~"), so claims were taken from cleanly extracted verbal sentences.

## Relevance to this manuscript
- M2: original source of the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure used for FDR control at 5% (q* = 0.05) within each pre-specified test family; supports the definition of FDR, the family-wise application (the paper itself applies the procedure within one of four pre-defined hypothesis families), and the power rationale over Bonferroni-type FWER control. The independence assumption should be acknowledged where test statistics within a family are correlated.

## Decision
KEEP — original description of the FDR-controlling step-up procedure used in all multiple-testing families.
